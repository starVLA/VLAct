Abstract

Scaling robot data is crucial for building generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, yet robot trajectories are fundamentally harder to scale than web-scale image-text data because they require embodied collection and sparsely cover the physical world. This makes representation quality a central bottleneck: under a fixed robot-data budget, VLA pre-training must convert limited trajectories into transferable visual-action knowledge, rather than merely fit actions.

We propose VLAct, a VLA-oriented VLM backbone trained with a representation-centric pre-training recipe. VLAct preserves the broad VLM prior, avoids over-specializing the backbone to a single action head, and encourages shared action semantics across embodiments through VLM-prior preservation, multi-head continuous action co-supervision, and a partially unified cross-embodiment action layout, while leaving downstream users free to attach task-specific action heads during fine-tuning.

Across multi-embodiment simulation benchmarks, real-world robot experiments, and unseen-embodiment transfer, VLAct consistently improves downstream performance under fixed fine-tuning protocols. On LIBERO-Plus and RoboTwin 2.0, VLAct surpasses large-scale industrial VLA systems such as ABot-M0 and LingBot-VLA, achieving 82.6% and 92.5% success, respectively. On the recently published RoboDojo simulation benchmark, VLAct achieves a 10.66 average score and 7.60% success rate, ranking sixth among all policies by success rate. It outperforms all explicitly designated world-action-model (WAM) entries on both metrics, as well as several industry-developed systems. Most notably, on RoboCasa-GR1, a humanoid embodiment never seen during pre-training, VLAct with only 20% of downstream trajectories already outperforms the full-data GR00T-N1.6 baseline. These results are obtained using fully open-source data and only a 16-GPU training setup, showing that representation-centric pre-training can deliver highly competitive performance under a modest compute budget and is an important independent axis of VLA progress beyond data scaling.

All models and training pipelines will be open-sourced.

Why look beyond data scaling?

Scaling data, model size, and training compute has been a central driver behind modern foundation models. In language and vision, this scaling paradigm succeeds not simply because web-scale corpora are large, but because they provide broad coverage of visual and semantic variation. VLA models naturally aspire to the same paradigm: more robot trajectories should, in principle, support more generalist robot policies. Yet robot learning faces a qualitatively different scaling bottleneck.

Robot trajectories cannot be scraped from the web. They must be produced through embodied execution in the physical world, often under human teleoperation or carefully designed data-collection protocols. More importantly, the space that robot policies must generalize over is combinatorial and continuous, spanning scenes, objects, task goals, embodiments, and contact-rich dynamics. As a result, even very large robot datasets remain sparse samples of the physical interaction space.

Given a fixed robot-data budget, how can VLA pre-training learn strong representations that generalize beyond the trajectories on which they were trained?

This paper takes a representation-centric view of VLA pre-training. Rather than treating robot pre-training merely as large-scale action fitting, we view it as a process of distilling robot trajectories into reusable visual-action knowledge within the backbone. A useful VLA backbone should not only predict the actions observed in the pre-training data, but also internalize transferable priors about objects, affordances, spatial relations, and action-conditioned physical interactions that downstream policies can reuse across new tasks, scenes, and embodiments.

We identify three failure modes in naive VLA pre-training. First, because robot trajectories are much narrower than the web-scale corpora used to train VLMs, full end-to-end updating can erode broadly useful vision-language features. Second, supervision from a single action head can over-specialize the backbone to that head's decoding geometry, limiting transfer to alternative heads. Third, embodiment-specific output spaces can weaken cross-embodiment sharing by representing physically comparable actions, such as gripper opening and closing, through isolated robot-specific heads.

Pilot Study: How Action Supervision Shapes VLA Backbones

A central goal of VLA pre-training is to produce a reusable backbone, rather than a policy initialization tied to one particular action head. In practice there is no universally optimal action head: discrete token heads (FAST), regression heads (OFT), flow-matching heads (PI), and diffusion-style continuous heads (GR00T) impose different inductive biases. We isolate this action-head axis through controlled pilot experiments on LIBERO-Plus and the RoboTwin-Clean setting. Fixing the VLM backbone to Qwen3-VL-4B, we vary the action head used during pre-training and fine-tuning, and test how different heads reshape the resulting backbone.

Action supervision reshapes VLA backbone representations

Keeping the VLM backbone fixed, we vary the action head used during pre-training and fine-tuning. Discrete FAST supervision can transfer to a continuous GR00T head, but retaining the FAST head suffers from discretization-induced information loss. Continuous OFT supervision improves same-head fine-tuning, but transfers poorly to other continuous heads, suggesting head-specific representation collapse rather than a generally reusable action representation.

Discrete supervision transfers, but loses information

FAST supervision can inject transferable structure into the backbone: paired with a continuous GR00T head, it slightly improves over from-scratch GR00T fine-tuning (76.7 vs. 75.9). But retaining the FAST head performs far worse than continuous-head fine-tuning (45.2 against 75.9 for a from-scratch GR00T head), and FAST pre-training does not close that gap — it even falls below a FAST head trained from scratch (61.4). Discrete action tokens teach coarse action structure while losing the fine-grained temporal and amplitude information that matters for manipulation.

Single-head continuous supervision collapses the geometry

OFT pre-training substantially improves downstream performance when the fine-tuning head is also OFT (75.8 vs. 61.7). This gain does not transfer: the same backbone drops to 55.1 with a PI head and 28.9 with a GR00T head. Action information may remain present, but organized in a head-specific form other decoders cannot easily read. Strong same-head performance therefore overstates backbone reusability.

Analysis Action supervision is not neutral: the pre-training head directly shapes the backbone representation. A transferable VLA backbone should preserve fine-grained action information while supporting multiple downstream heads — which motivates VLAct's multi-head continuous co-supervision.

VLAct: Representation-Centric VLA Pre-training

VLAct pre-trains a reusable VLA backbone from a VLM initialization. During pre-training, it preserves the VLM prior by freezing the vision encoder and shallow LLM layers, while mixing robot trajectories with caption data. Robot samples supervise the shared backbone through multiple continuous action heads — OFT, PI, and GR00T — under a partially unified action layout with masked inactive dimensions and wrap-aware losses. These components are used only to shape the backbone: during fine-tuning we discard the pre-training heads and caption stream, attach a freshly initialized task-specific action head, and train under the same downstream setting as each baseline.

① Preserve the VLM representation

We freeze the entire vision encoder and the lower half of the LLM layers during VLA pre-training, while updating the upper LLM layers and the action heads. This protects low-level visual processing and early vision-language alignment while letting upper layers adapt to action-conditioned reasoning. Caption-mixed pre-training additionally anchors the backbone with dense supervision over objects, attributes, spatial relations, and scene context. Freezing alone is worth +3.7% on LIBERO-Plus and +3.4% on AgileX.

② Make action features reusable

We attach three representative continuous heads — OFT, PI, and GR00T — to the shared backbone. Given the same vision-language input, the backbone produces one latent z; each head receives the same z and predicts the same ground-truth action chunk a, so the objective is simply L_OFT + L_PI + L_GR00T. Because all heads share one backbone forward pass, this adds only lightweight head-specific computation.

③ Align embodiments only where semantics match

Gripper dimensions are shared across embodiments because open/close commands have comparable semantics, while arm dimensions remain embodiment-specific when the robots differ in kinematics or degrees of freedom. Each sample contributes loss only on the active dimensions for its embodiment; inactive dimensions are masked out. No embodiment adapter, routing module, or embodiment-conditioned decoder is introduced.

④ Transfer only the backbone

The pre-training heads and auxiliary losses act as scaffolding: they shape the backbone representation but are not part of the downstream model. For each downstream task the desired continuous action head is attached and initialized from scratch, so improvements reflect the learned representation rather than a pre-adapted head.

Partially unified cross-embodiment action space

Different embodiments have different action spaces, and the common solution — one head or output projector per robot type — hides shared action structure inside embodiment-specific heads. VLAct instead uses one shared head over a 20-dimensional layout assigned by embodiment role: dims 1–6 and 7–12 are the left and right 6-DoF arms of bimanual robots (absolute joint angles), dims 13–18 the 6-DoF delta end-effector pose of single-arm robots, dim 19 the shared gripper coordinate, and dim 20 the right gripper. Each corpus keeps its native low-level action convention.

Wrap-aware loss Standard regression treats 179° and −179° as 358° apart, though they are physically only 2° apart. VLAct wraps absolute joint angles into [−π, π] and measures the residual modulo 2π before the L1 penalty. The correction applies only to periodic joint dimensions — gripper commands and delta end-effector translations are excluded.

Experiments

VLAct is pre-trained on fully open-source robot datasets — DROID, InternData-A1, RoboCoin, and MolmoAct — together with captioning data for VLM representation preservation. We build on the StarVLA training codebase and use Qwen3-VL-4B as the base vision-language backbone. All experiments run on 16 GPUs.

To isolate the effect of backbone representation quality, we keep the downstream action head, data budget, and fine-tuning recipe fixed, and replace only the pretrained backbone. Under this controlled protocol, VLAct consistently improves over generic VLM backbones and naive VLA-pretrained baselines.

Does VLAct transfer to unseen embodiments?

VLAct is pre-trained only on Franka single-arm and AgileX bimanual data. Both the GR-1 humanoid and the ARX X5 bimanual platform are held out during pre-training, so these settings directly probe cross-embodiment transfer: the target robots differ in morphology, action space, and manipulation dynamics.

RoboCasa-GR1: 20% of the data beats full-data baselines

With full fine-tuning data VLAct reaches 54.0%, above Qwen3VL-OFT (48.8%), GR00T-N1.6 (47.6%), and π0.5 (37.0%). The data-efficiency curve is the stronger result: with only 20% of RoboCasa-GR1 trajectories VLAct already reaches 49.5%, matching or exceeding the full-data Qwen3VL-OFT baseline; 50% of the data gives 51.0%, and full data 54.0%. VLAct is therefore not merely benefiting from downstream data scale — the pretrained backbone transfers useful action structure to the unseen embodiment before full task-specific adaptation.

RoboDojo: top quartile on an external leaderboard

RoboDojo evaluates policies on 42 ARX X5 simulation tasks spanning Generalization, Precision, Long-Horizon, Memory, and Open capabilities, 50 episodes each. Among 35 policies on the official leaderboard (August 24, 2026 snapshot), VLAct ranks eighth by average score and sixth by average success rate. It exceeds the strongest designated world-action model, X-WAM, by 2.97 score points and 3.77 percentage points of success, and outperforms Xiaomi-Robotics-0, GalaxeaVLA (G0), LingBot-VLA, and ABot-M0. Six of the seven higher-scoring entries come from industry teams. Against the Qwen3-VL-based StarVLA-α entry, VLAct gains 4.26 score points and 4.36 points of success, mostly on Precision and Long-Horizon; Memory remains a clear limitation.

What happens on real robots?

The physical evaluation platform uses stationary, table-mounted Franka Research 3 7-DoF arms — one for single-arm setups, two for dual-arm tasks. A fixed Intel RealSense D435 provides the third-person view and each arm carries a wrist-mounted RealSense D405; all images are resized to 224×224. We train two models, one for all single-arm tasks and one for all dual-arm tasks, each for 50k steps on 8 H800 GPUs, and roll out 10 trials per task. The baseline is Qwen3VL-4B-OFT without pre-training.

In-domain and novel-object short-horizon

On in-domain single-arm short-horizon tasks VLAct averages 92.5% success versus 77.5% for the baseline, with the clearest gains on spatially sensitive tasks — cube stacking (90% vs. 60%) and pen-in-cup (80% vs. 60%). Under object-level distribution shift it reaches 90.0% on both "novel object from pot" and "novel object in cup", against 73.3% and 65.0%.

Long-horizon execution

VLAct obtains weighted success rates of 86.6% on table cleaning and 80.0% on scooping beans, versus 73.3% and 33.3% for the baseline — which frequently skips the scooping phase and pours with an empty spoon. Under harder OOD settings VLAct keeps 82.5% on extended-sequence table cleaning and 83.3% under full object substitution, while the baseline drops to 47.5% and 46.6%.

Dual-arm coordination from single-arm pre-training

Despite being pre-trained only on single-arm data, VLAct reaches 72.0% average success across dual-arm tasks versus 44.0% for the baseline. The largest gaps appear on deformable-object and synchronization-heavy tasks: fold pants 70% vs. 40%, fold towel 50% vs. 20%, where the baseline suffers velocity mismatch between the two arms.

What this suggests

Representation-centric pre-training improves not only in-domain manipulation accuracy, but also novel-object generalization, long-horizon execution, and dual-arm coordination — the three regimes where the non-pre-trained backbone degrades most.

① Which auxiliary data preserves the prior?

LIBERO-Plus, with robot data, model, and pre-training budget fixed. Every auxiliary source beats robot-only training; image captions give the strongest single-source gain, taking 75.0 → 82.6. Even the off-domain, text-only instruction source improves on robot-only training, which is why the paper reads the effect as representation preservation rather than task transfer.

Takeaway

We presented VLAct, a representation-centric VLA pre-training recipe for building more transferable VLM backbones from limited robot data. Instead of treating robot pre-training as large-scale action fitting, VLAct preserves the broad VLM prior, avoids over-specialization to a single action head, and encourages shared action semantics across embodiments.

It surpasses strong VLA baselines on LIBERO-Plus, achieves state-of-the-art performance on RoboTwin 2.0, ranks in the top quartile of the RoboDojo leaderboard while outperforming several industry-developed systems, and rapidly adapts to a humanoid embodiment unseen during pre-training on RoboCasa-GR1. These results are obtained using fully open-source data and only a 16-GPU training setup. Their competitiveness against predominantly industry-backed systems shows that VLA progress is not only a matter of scaling robot trajectories or compute: better pre-training design can substantially improve what a fixed resource budget delivers.

Limitations. Due to resource constraints, our study focuses on a 4B-scale VLM backbone and does not explore substantially larger models. Larger VLMs may contain stronger visual, spatial, and language priors, and the optimal pre-training recipe may change with model scale.
